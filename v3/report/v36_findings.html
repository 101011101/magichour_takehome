Why ER, and why nothing more

The short version: the reference is the ceiling, and the reference cannot be improved by generating it. Everything here is how the v3.x series arrived there, why every more ambitious arm lost, and why one verb in call 2 is the most that can be taken. ← the result

1. The architecture, as deployed

call 1klein bald pass

The garment photograph, edited to remove the wearer's hair. A small, in-distribution edit — the only generative step that touches the source.

→
no modelV2 cropper, head subtracted

BiRefNet matte × human-parser classes, cranium → white. Deterministic and subtractive only: it can remove, never invent. The garment pixels that reach call 2 are the photograph's.

→
call 2klein edit — ER

Person + reference in, dressed person out. The one line this investigation changed.

BCDress the person in image 1 in the clothing shown in image 2. Keep the person's face, identity, body and the background exactly as they are.
ERReplace the clothing in image 1 with the clothing in image 2. Keep the person's face, identity, body and the background exactly as they are.

Two calls, one deterministic stage between them. Call 1 turns a photograph of a person wearing clothes into a reference that shows the clothes without the identity; call 2 puts that reference on the target. The series' central finding is that call 1 decides the outcome — call 2 transfers whatever it is given, faithfully, including the mistakes.

2. Regeneration is a tax, and it is charged on the garment

v3.1 to v3.4 built increasingly sophisticated call-1 arms: mannequin head swaps, explicit re-posing, canvas discipline, super-resolution applied only to finished references. The best of them, VEi, is the v3.4 lock. Measured over the same 600 cells it failed 9.2% against the incumbent's 4.8% — 43 cells where the sophisticated arm fails and the simple one does not, against 17 the other way.

The mechanism: BC edits the head and isolates the garment with a deterministic matte, which can only remove. Every V-family arm asks a generative model to re-draw the whole frame, because a head swap and a re-pose are full-frame edits. A distilled 4-step model re-drawing a garment does not copy it — it resamples it, and what it loses is exactly what call 2 needs: weave, print registration, seam and hem placement, the count of pieces. The loss is not noise, it is structured invention, and call 2 cannot tell an invented placket from a real one. A call-1 defect is not carried forward, it is amplified.

The load-bearing corollary: re-folding, unfolding, flattening or otherwise restyling the garment through klein does not work — not because the prompt is wrong, but because the operation is generative, and every generative touch of the garment costs fidelity that call 2 then magnifies. Compaction schemes fail identically: re-encoding the garment into a cleaner or canonical form is a lossy re-encode, and the thing lost is the fidelity the second call is about to amplify. If a genuinely restructured reference is ever needed it must come from a different model — QX, v3.1's qwen-image-edit garment isolation — not from a better prompt to this one.

Which leaves exactly one safe edit: the one that removes. A small in-distribution edit to the head, then a matte that subtracts. That is the whole of the deployed pipeline, and it is why it looks under-ambitious.

4. The limit, made concrete — hands

The clearest residual defect is a reference whose wearer has arms crossed. The matte cannot know the folded arms are not part of the garment's shape, so call 2 receives a jacket with a pair of arms baked into its silhouette, and duplicates them.

p008+dualuse_emma_watson_black_blazer_armscrossedseed 47arms crossed in the reference

The tempting fix is to unfold the arms in call 1 — precisely the operation §3 rules out. Unfolding is a full-frame regeneration: to remove the duplicated arms it must re-draw the sleeves, the front closure and the drape, which is where lengths change and pieces vanish. The fix costs more than the defect.

V2's dynamic-prompt rule — never name a body part the crop excludes — had governed call 1 since v3.1 on an assumption. v3.6 measured it on call 2: on the 59 cells whose photograph has no feet in frame, running the same pose read over the outputs finds invented feet in 21 of ERS's cells and 2 of ERD's. Naming an absent limb makes the model draw it, twelvefold — and that is also the explanation for the EX reframing. The conclusion is not "use the dynamic clause" but use no clause: plain ER never names a limb, so it never triggers the failure, and it costs no pose read.

Two controls make all of the above readable. On the 86 cells where ERD and ERS were sent identical text the outputs are byte-identical, so every difference measured is the prompt and not sampling noise. And fal is not the A100: same prompt, seed and canvas rule, yet most archived failures do not reproduce there — so every number of record was made on the hardware the record was made on.

6. The seed randomiser — what the arithmetic actually says

Every pair in the sweep was run at three seeds, so the record itself says what a retry would have done. For ER: a failed cell passes at another seed 26/36 = 72% of the time, which takes 3.00% to 0.83%; using both remaining seeds reaches 0.50%, the floor set by the one pair in two hundred that fails at every seed.

Why it is not higher. If seeds were independent at a 3.00% failure rate, a retry would pass 97% of the time and the residual would be 0.09%. It is 72% instead, so failures cluster by pair: when a cell fails, the same pair is likelier to fail at other seeds too. The randomiser works, but nothing like as well as independence would predict — a stubborn pair is stubborn because its reference is wrong, and no seed repairs that.

Why ER compounds with it: its failures are less clustered. That is the property the randomiser actually runs on, and it is separate from the rate. When a pair fails at all, BC fails at 1.53 of its three seeds on average and 42% of the time at two or more; ER fails at 1.29 and only 21%. So a retry meets another failed seed 28% of the time on ER against 41% on BC. A lower rate and a looser cluster multiply rather than overlap, which is why the shipped system is the prompt and the policy rather than either alone.

What the retry does and does not change. A randomised seed does not move the 3.00%: a first draw is a random cell, and 3.00% of cells fail. What the policy buys is the second draw — 3.00% × 28% = 0.83% of images still failing after one retry, at 1.03 calls per delivered image.

Estimated on 36 retry opportunities, so the 95% interval on that 72% is roughly 56–84%. The direction is solid; the second decimal is not. And the policy has an unbuilt dependency: a rejector. Something must decide an image failed before it can be retried. The v3.6 VLM judge is a first attempt and is not yet one — its artifact flag fires on 45% of cells a human passed, though its limb flag (14.8× lift over base rate) and phasing (2.7×) are the signals to build on.

7. Where this leaves the work

  • Ship ER plus the retry. 4.83% → 3.00% → about 0.83%, for one verb and ~3% more calls.
  • Build the rejector. It is the only thing standing between the measured 0.83% and a shipped one.
  • The remaining defects are reference-side, which means model-side: crossed arms, dropped pieces on ambiguous lower bodies, the wearer's own accessories. None is a call-2 wording problem.
  • The open architectural question is not "is BC better" but does re-posing buy back more than the re-draw costs, and on what share of a real catalogue? This fold is mostly front-facing wearers, which flatters the simple arm.
  • The arm nobody has built is the router: re-pose only when a pose reader says the wearer is not neutral, otherwise ship the garment pixels untouched. That is the shape the evidence recommends, and it is not a prompt.
